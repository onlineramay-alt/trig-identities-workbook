Worked Examples · Trigonometrical Identities

Four techniques, worked in full

Every ICSE-style trig identity question is really one of four jobs: prove a result that follows from a given condition, remove θ from a pair of equations, solve an equation down to a named angle, or read a length off a right triangle built from an angle of elevation or depression. Twenty-eight examples below — six per technique, ten for heights and distances, running from warm-up to full board-exam difficulty — each carried step by step from the given to the result.

A. Establishing Conditional Results

A relation between the ratios is handed to you — use it, don't re-derive it.

Method. The given condition is a tool, not a target. Either (1) solve it for one ratio and substitute that into the expression to be proved, or (2) combine the condition algebraically with itself (square it, multiply by its "conjugate") until a Pythagorean identity appears and cancels a variable out.
A.1Warm-up

If sin θ + cosec θ = 2, prove that sin²θ + cosec²θ = 2.

Let s = sin θ. The condition reads s + 1/s = 2.
Multiply through by s: s² − 2s + 1 = 0, i.e. (s − 1)² = 0.
A perfect square forces the value — this is what makes the condition worth using.
So s = 1, meaning sin θ = 1 and cosec θ = 1 both hold exactly.
sin²θ + cosec²θ = 1² + 1² = 2 ∎
A.2Standard

If x = r sin θ cos φ, y = r sin θ sin φ, z = r cos θ, prove that x² + y² + z² = r².

x² + y² = r²sin²θcos²φ + r²sin²θsin²φ = r²sin²θ(cos²φ + sin²φ)
cos²φ + sin²φ = 1, so x² + y² = r²sin²θ.
Now bring in z²= r²cos²θ: x²+y²+z² = r²sin²θ + r²cos²θ = r²(sin²θ+cos²θ)
= r² ∎
Two applications of sin²+cos²=1 — once in φ, once in θ.
A.3Standard

If a cos θ − b sin θ = c, prove that a sin θ + b cos θ = ±√(a² + b² − c²).

Name the given quantity u = a cosθ − b sinθ = c, and let v = a sinθ + b cosθ be the unknown.
Expand u² + v² — the cross terms −2ab sinθcosθ and +2ab sinθcosθ cancel:
u² + v² = a²(cos²θ+sin²θ) + b²(sin²θ+cos²θ) = a² + b²
This cancellation is the whole trick: u²+v² is independent of θ.
Substitute u = c: c² + v² = a² + b² → v² = a² + b² − c²
a sin θ + b cos θ = v = ±√(a² + b² − c²) ∎
A.4Standard

If sec θ + tan θ = p, prove that sin θ = (p² − 1)/(p² + 1).

(sec θ + tan θ)(sec θ − tan θ) = sec²θ − tan²θ = 1, so sec θ − tan θ = 1/p.
Add the two equations: 2 sec θ = p + 1/p = (p²+1)/p
Subtract them: 2 tan θ = p − 1/p = (p²−1)/p
sin θ = tan θ / sec θ = [(p²−1)/p] ÷ [(p²+1)/p]
sin θ = (p² − 1)/(p² + 1) ∎
A.5Challenge

If cos θ + sin θ = √2 cos θ, prove that cos θ − sin θ = √2 sin θ.

Rearrange the given relation: sin θ = (√2 − 1) cos θ.
The claim to prove is equivalent to cos θ = (√2 + 1) sin θ. Substitute the line above into its right side:
(√2 + 1) sin θ = (√2 + 1)(√2 − 1) cos θ = (2 − 1) cos θ = cos θ
(√2+1)(√2−1) is a difference of squares, 2 − 1 — the surd vanishes cleanly.
The right side reduces to exactly cos θ, so cos θ − sin θ = √2 sin θ ∎
A.6Challenge

If x sin³θ + y cos³θ = sin θ cos θ and x sin θ = y cos θ, prove that x² + y² = 1.

From the second condition, x sin θ = y cos θ, so x = y cos θ / sin θ (sin θ ≠ 0).
Substitute into the first condition:
(y cos θ / sin θ) sin³θ + y cos³θ = sin θ cos θ
y cos θ sin²θ + y cos³θ = sin θ cos θ
Factor out y cos θ: y cos θ (sin²θ + cos²θ) = sin θ cos θ → y cos θ = sin θ cos θ
So y = sin θ (cos θ ≠ 0). Then from x sin θ = y cos θ = sin θ cos θ, we get x = cos θ.
x² + y² = cos²θ + sin²θ = 1 ∎

B. Elimination of the Unknown Angle

Two equations trace out x and y as θ varies — find the one relation between x and y that no longer mentions θ.

Method. Isolate a trig ratio of θ on one side of each equation (x/a = cos θ, y/b = sin θ, …), then combine using a Pythagorean identity so θ cancels entirely. Squaring both equations and adding is the move that works more often than any other.
B.1Warm-up

If x = r cos θ and y = r sin θ, eliminate θ.

x² = r²cos²θ and y² = r²sin²θ.
x² + y² = r²(cos²θ + sin²θ) = r² — θ has disappeared.
x² + y² = r² (a circle of radius r) ∎
B.2Warm-up

If x = a cos θ and y = b sin θ, eliminate θ.

x/a = cos θ and y/b = sin θ.
Square both and add: (x/a)² + (y/b)² = cos²θ + sin²θ
x²/a² + y²/b² = 1 (an ellipse) ∎
B.3Standard

If x = a sec θ and y = b tan θ, eliminate θ.

x/a = sec θ and y/b = tan θ.
Use sec²θ − tan²θ = 1, not sin²+cos²=1 — the ratios here are sec and tan.
(x/a)² − (y/b)² = sec²θ − tan²θ
x²/a² − y²/b² = 1 (a hyperbola) ∎
B.4Standard

If x cos θ + y sin θ = p and x sin θ − y cos θ = q, eliminate θ.

Square both equations and add them.
(x cosθ+y sinθ)² = x²cos²θ + 2xy sinθcosθ + y²sin²θ
(x sinθ−y cosθ)² = x²sin²θ − 2xy sinθcosθ + y²cos²θ
The 2xy sinθcosθ cross terms carry opposite signs and cancel on adding.
Sum = x²(cos²θ+sin²θ) + y²(sin²θ+cos²θ) = x² + y²
x² + y² = p² + q² ∎
B.5Standard

If x = a(cos θ + sin θ) and y = a(cos θ − sin θ), eliminate θ.

x² + y² = a²[(cosθ+sinθ)² + (cosθ−sinθ)²]
(cosθ+sinθ)² = 1 + 2sinθcosθ and (cosθ−sinθ)² = 1 − 2sinθcosθ — the cross terms cancel on adding.
x² + y² = a²[2cos²θ + 2sin²θ] = 2a²
x² + y² = 2a² ∎
B.6Challenge

If x = a cos³θ and y = b sin³θ, eliminate θ.

(x/a) = cos³θ and (y/b) = sin³θ, so (x/a)^(1/3) = cos θ and (y/b)^(1/3) = sin θ.
Cube roots first — Pythagoras needs cos θ and sin θ themselves, not their cubes.
Square each and add: (x/a)^(2/3) + (y/b)^(2/3) = cos²θ + sin²θ
(x/a)^(2/3) + (y/b)^(2/3) = 1 (an astroid) ∎

C. Finding the Unknown Angle

Solve the equation down to one ratio of one angle, then read θ off the standard table. Domain: 0° ≤ θ ≤ 90° throughout.

Method. Reduce to a single trig ratio — by direct algebra, by factoring a quadratic in sin θ / cos θ / tan θ, or by matching two different angles with sin θ = cos(90° − θ). Then read θ from the standard-values table, and check every root actually lies in the stated domain before keeping it.
C.1Warm-up

Solve for θ: 2 sin θ − √3 = 0.

sin θ = √3/2
θ = 60° ∎
C.2Warm-up

Solve for θ: sec θ − √2 = 0.

sec θ = √2 → cos θ = 1/√2
θ = 45° ∎
C.3Standard

Solve for θ: 2 sin²θ − sin θ − 1 = 0.

This is a quadratic in sin θ. Let s = sin θ: 2s² − s − 1 = 0
Factor: (2s + 1)(s − 1) = 0 → s = −1/2 or s = 1
Reject s = −1/2: on 0°–90°, sin θ never goes negative.
So sin θ = 1
θ = 90° ∎
C.4Standard

Solve for θ: sin 3θ = cos(θ + 6°), where 3θ and θ + 6° are both acute.

Rewrite the left side as a cosine so both sides name the same function: sin 3θ = cos(90° − 3θ)
cos(90° − 3θ) = cos(θ + 6°)
Equal cosines of acute angles ⇒ the angles themselves are equal.
90° − 3θ = θ + 6° → 84° = 4θ
θ = 21° (check: 3θ = 63°, θ+6° = 27°, both acute ✓) ∎
C.5Challenge

Solve for θ: tan²θ − (1 + √3) tan θ + √3 = 0.

A quadratic in tan θ with two standard-angle roots built in. Factor directly:
(tan θ − 1)(tan θ − √3) = 0
tan θ = 1 or tan θ = √3
θ = 45° or θ = 60° ∎
C.6Challenge

Solve for θ: sec²θ = 1 + √3 tan θ.

Rewrite sec²θ using 1 + tan²θ = sec²θ, so both sides speak the same ratio:
1 + tan²θ = 1 + √3 tan θ → tan²θ − √3 tan θ = 0
Factor: tan θ (tan θ − √3) = 0 → tan θ = 0 or tan θ = √3
θ = 0° or θ = 60° ∎

D. Heights and Distances

Every diagram is one right triangle (or two, joined by a shared side) — the trigonometry never gets harder than SOH CAH TOA.

Method. Draw the triangle before touching a formula: mark the right angle, the given length, and the given angle. Decide whether you have/want the opposite, adjacent, or hypotenuse relative to that angle and pick tan, sin, or cos accordingly. An angle of elevation is measured upward from the horizontal; an angle of depression downward — and by alternate angles between the two parallel horizontals, the depression angle at the top equals the elevation angle at the point below. Two unknowns from one point needs two equations — eliminate the shared distance and solve for the height.
D.1Warm-up

From a point on the ground 25 m from the foot of a tower, the angle of elevation of its top is 30°. Find the height of the tower, correct to two decimal places.

Let the height be h. The tower is opposite the 30° angle; the 25 m is adjacent to it — that's tangent.
tan 30° = h/25 → 1/√3 = h/25
h = 25/√3 = 25√3/3 = 25 × 1.732 / 3
h = 14.43 m ∎
Check: the angle is under 45°, so the height should be less than the base — 14.43 < 25 ✓
D.2Warm-up

From the top of a cliff 50 m high, the angle of depression of a buoy is 30°. Find the distance of the buoy from the foot of the cliff, correct to two decimal places.

By alternate angles between the horizontal at the top and the horizontal sea surface, the angle of elevation from the buoy to the top is also 30°.
Let the distance be d. The cliff (50 m) is opposite the angle; d is adjacent — tangent again.
tan 30° = 50/d → 1/√3 = 50/d → d = 50√3
d = 50 × 1.732 = 86.60 m ∎
D.3Standard

The angles of elevation of the top of a tower from two points on level ground, in line with its foot, are 60° and 45°. If the two points are 25 m apart, find the height of the tower, correct to two decimal places.

Let the height be h, the foot B, the nearer point C (60°) and the farther point D (45°), with CD = 25 m.
From C: tan 60° = h/BC → BC = h/√3
From D: tan 45° = h/BD → BD = h
BD = BC + CD, so h = h/√3 + 25 → h(1 − 1/√3) = 25
h × 0.4226 = 25 → h = 59.15
Height ≈ 59.15 m ∎
Two angles from two points → two equations in h and one shared distance; eliminate the distance, solve for h. That's the whole pattern.
D.4Standard

From the top of a lighthouse 60 m high, the angles of depression of two boats on opposite sides are 60° and 30°. Find the distance between the boats, correct to two decimal places.

Both depression angles transfer to elevation angles at the boats, by alternate angles — one triangle on each side of the lighthouse.
Nearer boat (60°): tan 60° = 60/d₁ → d₁ = 60/√3 = 20√3 = 34.64 m
Farther boat (30°): tan 30° = 60/d₂ → d₂ = 60√3 = 103.92 m
The two triangles sit on opposite sides of the foot, so the distances ADD rather than subtract.
Distance between boats = 34.64 + 103.92 = 138.56 m ∎
D.5Challenge

A flagpole stands on top of a building. From a point 30 m from the foot of the building, the angles of elevation of the foot and the top of the flagpole are 45° and 60°. Find the height of the flagpole, correct to two decimal places.

Two triangles share the same base, 30 m — one to the foot of the flagpole (top of the building), one to its top.
Building height: tan 45° = h/30 → h = 30 m
Building + flagpole: tan 60° = H/30 → H = 30√3 = 51.96 m
The flagpole itself is the difference of the two heights, not either one alone — a common slip is stopping at H.
Flagpole height = 51.96 − 30 = 21.96 m ∎
D.6Challenge

A man finds the angle of elevation of the top of a tower to be 30°. He walks 40 m towards it on level ground, and the angle becomes 60°. Find the height of the tower, correct to two decimal places.

Let the height be h, and let x be the distance from the tower at the second (nearer) position.
Nearer position: tan 60° = h/x → x = h/√3
Farther position, 40 m further back: tan 30° = h/(x + 40) → x + 40 = h√3
Substitute x: h/√3 + 40 = h√3 → multiply by √3: h + 40√3 = 3h → 2h = 40√3
h = 20√3 = 20 × 1.732
Height ≈ 34.64 m ∎
D.7Challenge

From the top of a building 20 m high, the angle of elevation of the top of a tower is 30°, and the angle of depression of the foot of the tower is 45°. Find the height of the tower, correct to two decimal places.

Draw a horizontal line from the top of the building (A) across to the tower. It meets the tower at a point E level with A, splitting the tower into ED (below, level with the ground) and EC (above, up to the tower's top).
ED = AB = 20 m (the building's own height), and AE = the horizontal gap between building and tower — call it x.
Depression to the foot D is 45°: tan 45° = ED/AE = 20/x → x = 20 (since tan 45° = 1)
Elevation to the top C is 30°: tan 30° = EC/AE = EC/20 → EC = 20/√3 = 11.55
The tower's total height is ED + EC, not EC alone — the 20 m below the horizontal line still counts.
Tower height = 20 + 11.55 = 31.55 m ∎
D.8Challenge

A 10 m ladder leans against a wall, making an angle of 60° with the ground. The foot is then pulled away until the ladder makes an angle of 30° with the ground instead. Find how far the foot was pulled out, and how far the top slides down the wall, correct to two decimal places.

The ladder's length (10 m) is the hypotenuse throughout — only the angle changes. Foot distance uses cosine, height on the wall uses sine.
At 60°: foot distance = 10 cos 60° = 10 × 1/2 = 5 m. Height up the wall = 10 sin 60° = 10 × √3/2 = 8.66 m.
At 30°: foot distance = 10 cos 30° = 10 × √3/2 = 8.66 m. Height up the wall = 10 sin 30° = 10 × 1/2 = 5 m.
cos 60° = sin 30° and cos 30° = sin 60° — complementary angles swap sine and cosine, which is why both changes come out to exactly the same number here.
Foot pulled out = 8.66 − 5 = 3.66 m. Top slides down = 8.66 − 5 = 3.66 m.
Foot moves out 3.66 m; top slides down 3.66 m ∎
D.9Challenge

Two vertical poles of heights 6 m and 11 m stand on level ground, 12 m apart. Find the distance between their tops, and the tangent of the angle of elevation of the taller pole's top as seen from the shorter pole's top.

Draw a horizontal line from the top of the shorter pole across to the taller one. It meets the taller pole 6 m up from its base, leaving 11 − 6 = 5 m of pole above that line.
This horizontal line, the leftover 5 m, and the segment joining the two tops form a right triangle with legs 12 m and 5 m.
Distance between tops = √(12² + 5²) = √(144 + 25) = √169
12–5–13 is a Pythagorean triple — no decimal rounding needed here.
tan θ = opposite/adjacent = 5/12, where θ is the elevation angle from the shorter pole's top
Distance between tops = 13 m; tan θ = 5/12 ∎
D.10Challenge

A tower is surmounted by a flagstaff 6 m tall. From a point on the ground, the angles of elevation of the top and the bottom of the flagstaff are 60° and 45° respectively. Find the height of the tower, correct to two decimal places.

Unlike D.5, neither the tower's height nor the distance to it is given — only the flagstaff's length. Let the tower height be h and the (unknown) horizontal distance be x.
To the bottom of the flagstaff (top of the tower), 45°: tan 45° = h/x → x = h
To the top of the flagstaff, 60°: tan 60° = (h + 6)/x → x = (h + 6)/√3
Set the two expressions for x equal: h = (h + 6)/√3 → h√3 = h + 6 → h(√3 − 1) = 6
h = 6/(√3 − 1). Rationalise by multiplying by (√3 + 1)/(√3 + 1):
h = 6(√3 + 1)/(3 − 1) = 3(√3 + 1) = 3√3 + 3
h = 3(1.732) + 3 = 8.20 m ∎
